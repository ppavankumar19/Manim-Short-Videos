This topic sits directly inside **Chapter 1: Reflection of Light at Curved Surfaces** in the uploaded Telangana Class 10 Physical Sciences textbook.

The textbook derives the relation

\[
\frac1f=\frac1v+\frac1u
\]

# **DON'T SUBSTITUTE FIRST. SIGN FIRST.**

# 1. Learning Objective

By the end of the video, students should be able to:

- identify the **pole \(P\)** as the reference point for all mirror distances;
- identify the direction of the **incident light**;
- assign positive and negative directions correctly according to the textbook convention;
- identify signed:
  - object distance \(u\),
  - image distance \(v\),
  - focal length \(f\);
- use

\[
\frac1f=\frac1v+\frac1u
\]

without losing signs;
- interpret the sign of the calculated \(v\);
- solve the textbook-style concave-mirror example:
  \[
  u=-25\text{ cm},\qquad f=-15\text{ cm};
  \]
- obtain:
  \[
  \boxed{v=-37.5\text{ cm}}.
  \]

The textbook explicitly states that all distances are measured from the **pole**, distances measured in the direction of incident light are positive, and those measured opposite to incident light are negative.

# 3. Core Visual Metaphor

## “The Mirror Number Line”

Keep one horizontal principal axis persistent through almost the entire video.

At the mirror's pole:

\[
\boxed{P=0}
\]

The direction in which incident light travels becomes:

# `POSITIVE DIRECTION`

The opposite side becomes:

# `NEGATIVE DIRECTION`

This is directly based on the textbook convention.

For the textbook's usual concave-mirror drawing, the object is to the right and incident rays travel toward the mirror to the left.

So animate:

\[
\longleftarrow\quad \text{Incident Light}
\]

Therefore:

### Left of \(P\)

\[
\boxed{+}
\]

because it is in the direction of incident light.

### Right of \(P\)

\[
\boxed{-}
\]

because it is opposite the direction of incident light.

That single visual rule should replace rote memorization.

# 4. Core Textbook Content

The textbook first derives the spherical-mirror equation from the ray geometry and reaches:

\[
uv=uf+vf.
\]

Dividing by \(uvf\), it obtains:

\[
\boxed{\frac1f=\frac1v+\frac1u}.
\]

It then gives the sign convention:

1. all distances are measured from the pole;
2. distances in the direction of incident light are positive;
3. distances opposite to incident light are negative;
4. heights above the principal axis are positive and heights below it are negative.

For this video, Rule 4 can appear briefly, while Rules 1–3 remain the main focus because the direct topic is \(u,v,f\).

# 7. Key Pedagogical Safeguards

### A. Follow the Telangana textbook's stated convention

For this production, use exactly:

\[
\boxed{\text{Direction of incident light}=+}
\]

\[
\boxed{\text{Opposite direction}=-}
\]

with all distances measured from the pole.

Do not silently replace this with a differently oriented sign diagram from another textbook.

---

### B. Do not teach “left = negative/right = positive”

That rule depends on how the optical diagram is drawn.

The invariant rule from this textbook is about the **direction of incident light**.

The animation should actually allow the whole mirror diagram to flip once briefly while the sign arrow remains attached to incident-light direction.

That proves the concept is directional, not screen-position memorization.

---

### C. Separate magnitude from signed distance

Given:

`Object is 25 cm in front of the mirror`

the physical magnitude is:

\[
25\text{ cm}.
\]

After applying direction:

\[
u=-25\text{ cm}.
\]

Students should visually see:

\[
\boxed{\text{Magnitude}}
+
\boxed{\text{Direction}}
=
\boxed{\text{Signed Distance}}
\]

---

### D. Never drop minus signs during reciprocal arithmetic

Keep:

\[
\frac1{-15}
\]

visually intact.

Then transform it to:

\[
-\frac1{15}.
\]

Likewise:

\[
\frac1{-25}
=
-\frac1{25}.
\]

This avoids a common algebraic error.

---

### E. The mirror formula uses signed \(u,v,f\)

Do not substitute absolute values and attempt to “fix the sign later.”

The textbook explicitly places sign convention immediately after the mirror formula and says it must be used in every situation.

---

### F. Interpret the final sign geometrically

After obtaining:

\[
v=-37.5\text{ cm},
\]

do not end the problem.

Return to the number line and place the image at the corresponding side of \(P\).

The algebra and ray diagram should agree.

---

### G. Keep magnification out of the main lesson

The textbook continues immediately into:

\[
m=-\frac vu
\]

and image-height relations.

But this storyboard should stop after the mirror-formula interpretation. That keeps the lesson focused on its real difficulty: **sign convention**.

A separate storyboard can handle magnification.
